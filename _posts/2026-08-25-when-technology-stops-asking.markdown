# When Technology Stops Asking

A conversation about trying new software somehow turned into one about cameras in earbuds. That is probably a fairly accurate summary of where consumer technology is heading.

We started out talking about trust. If a developer or project has consistently made good things, I am usually willing to try whatever they release next. There is a certain sense of adventure in doing that. Sometimes blind loyalty works out, sometimes it bites you, and somehow you still end up doing it again.

But that kind of trust is still a choice. I decide what to install, what device to buy, and which project gets the benefit of the doubt. The more interesting problem begins when technology no longer needs my choice at all.

---

# The device you did not buy

The idea of putting cameras into something as ordinary as earbuds bothers me. Not only because I do not personally need it, but because devices like that are designed to disappear into everyday life. A phone being held up is noticeable. Someone wearing earbuds is not.

The usual reasoning will probably be convenience, augmented reality, AI features, or some combination of all three. Point at something and ask what it is. Get directions without looking at a screen. Let an assistant understand the space around you. I can see why those features would be useful.

I can also see what they require. A device needs to constantly observe its surroundings before it can be helpful about them.

If I decide that tradeoff is not worth it, I can simply refuse to buy the product. At least that is how opting out is supposed to work. The problem is that everyone around me can still buy one. Their devices can record me, classify me, and create more data points about my existence without me ever agreeing to anything.

At that point it is no longer just their personal convenience. I have been pulled into the product too.

---

# Consent does not scale through other people

We already give away an absurd amount of information through phones, websites, payment systems, location services, and social platforms. Most of us know this, even if we try not to think about it too much. We accept it because opting out completely makes modern life considerably harder.

Ambient devices make that problem worse. The data collection no longer stops with the person who accepted the terms. One person's agreement quietly becomes everyone else's participation.

You cannot meaningfully consent to a device you do not own, software you cannot inspect, and a privacy policy you were never shown. You also cannot realistically ask every person near you whether their earbuds, glasses, or whatever comes next are recording.

That is the part I find unpleasant. It is not simply another company collecting data from its customers. It is the possibility of companies building datasets about people who were never customers in the first place, then using or selling those datasets for profit.

---

# Convenience is not the problem

I am not against new technology, and I am definitely not against experimenting with it. I work on software that people install on their systems, and I understand why useful features often need access to information. Technology can do genuinely interesting things when it understands more of its environment.

The difference is control.

When I run a tool, I want to know what it is doing. I want to decide when it is active, what it can access, and where the result goes. If I stop trusting it, I should be able to remove it and be done with it.

That relationship disappears when the sensors belong to everyone else. I can switch to a dumb phone and carry an MP3 player if I want. I can show up to a convention with a PSP and be perfectly happy about it. None of that protects me from a world filled with devices that observe first and ask questions later.

---

# The actual tradeoff

We often describe privacy as something we exchange for convenience. That makes it sound like a personal deal. You hand over some data, and in return you get a useful service.

But it stops being a personal deal when other people can make it on your behalf.

Maybe camera-equipped earbuds will be useful. Maybe they will be a pointless marketing gimmick. The specific product almost does not matter. What matters is the direction: more sensors, more passive collection, more AI integration, and fewer moments where anyone is clearly asked whether they want to participate.

I still like the sense of adventure that comes with trying something new. I just want the choice to be mine.
